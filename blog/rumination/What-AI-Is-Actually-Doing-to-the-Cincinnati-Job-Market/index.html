What AI Is Actually Doing to the Cincinnati Job Market

November 5, 2026

Everyone has an opinion about what AI is doing to jobs. Almost nobody has measured it for a specific place.

So I did, for Greater Cincinnati — all 1,101,030 jobs in the metro, joined to published AI exposure research and checked against what the region's largest employers have said in their own filings.

Cincinnati's AI exposure is average. The metro scores 0.088 against a national 0.093 — a gap of five thousandths of a standard deviation. Neither unusually threatened nor unusually safe.

The real vulnerability isn't exposure — it's that nothing is growing. Cincinnati added 8,600 jobs in two years, 0.7%, while the labour force shrank by 9,374. Of 101,316 projected annual openings, just 2,074 come from growth. The other 98% exist only because someone leaves. In an economy with no growth cushion, a technology that slows hiring attacks the only door into employment that exists.

The region is buying AI capability, not building it. Cincinnati has 13% fewer computing jobs than its size implies, and the biggest advertisers of AI roles here are national consultancies and staffing firms, not local employers.

Exposure rises with pay. It's negative across the bottom three wage quintiles and reaches +0.703 SD in the top. The most exposed jobs here are accountants, HR specialists and financial managers — not production workers.

What I won't claim: that AI has caused measurable job losses here. P&G attributes its 7,000-role cut to cost and tariffs. Banking fell 13.9% from 2019 but only 4.1% from 2024 — seven-tenths of that happened before generative AI existed.
